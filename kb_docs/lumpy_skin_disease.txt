Lumpy skin disease (LSD) in cattle and buffalo: starter notes for farmers.
What it is: LSD is a viral disease of cattle and buffalo caused by a capripox virus. It spreads mainly by biting insects such as flies, mosquitoes and ticks. It can also spread through close contact, shared water troughs, and by moving sick animals. It does not spread from cattle to people.
Signs: fever, loss of appetite, less milk, watery discharge from the eyes and nose, swollen glands near the shoulder and in front of the thigh, and firm round lumps in the skin, usually 2 to 5 centimetres wide, on the neck, back, belly, legs, udder and head. Lumps may break open and form scabs or sores. Legs can swell and the animal may limp.
What to do first: separate the sick animal from the other animals straight away and keep it in a shaded, clean place with water and soft feed. Call the veterinarian the same day. Report suspected cases to the local animal husbandry office, because LSD outbreaks are tracked by the government.
Control of insects: keep the animal shed clean and dry, remove standing water and dung heaps, use fly and mosquito control as advised by the vet, and keep sick animals in a fly-proof or screened place if possible.
Movement: do not take sick animals to markets or fairs, and do not mix new animals with the herd until a vet has checked them. Keep new animals apart for at least two weeks.
Vaccination: vaccination of healthy cattle and buffalo is the main protection. Governments run vaccination drives in affected areas. Ask your veterinarian about the vaccine and the right time for your animals. Vaccinate healthy animals only.
Care of the sick animal: only the vet should decide medicines. Sores should be kept clean as the vet shows you. Give clean water and soft, easy feed. Most animals recover over weeks, but milk yield can stay low, and young animals and weak animals are at higher risk.
Call the vet urgently if: the animal stops eating or drinking, has high fever, cannot stand, has swollen legs, or the sores smell bad or have maggots.
